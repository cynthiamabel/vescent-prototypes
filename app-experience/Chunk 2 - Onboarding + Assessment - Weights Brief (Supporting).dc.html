How should we weight the personality assessment?

A research-grounded look at what the personality signal is worth in our 5-signal match model — drawing on both the youth-mentoring literature and the organizational/workplace evidence. Prepared by Cynthia, Jul 20 2026 (v2).

The decision on the table

Our designed model weighs five signals: Responsiveness 30% · 4H Journey 25% · Interests 20% · Experiences 15% · Communication style 10%. Communication style is derived from the Jungian personality assessment, so personality currently carries only 10%. Both teens and mentors complete the assessment and receive a 4-letter type; our tool compares both sides.

Cynthia's challenge (and it's a good one): personality assessments are ubiquitous in corporate culture for a reason — understanding who you're working with can only help the relationship. Even if that research isn't about near-peer mentoring, it should inform our thinking. This v2 adds that evidence. The short version: it strengthens the case for personality — but points to a specific, higher-value role for it than a bigger matching weight.

The reframe, up front: across both bodies of research, personality assessment earns its keep primarily as a relationship tool — building self-awareness, mutual understanding, and communication — and only secondarily as a matching criterion. That suggests Vescent gets the most value by using personality in two ways: a moderate matching weight, plus giving the mentor her mentee's type as a communication aid. The second use is where the corporate evidence is strongest, and it's nearly free to build.

What the workplace / organizational research says

Personality tools (MBTI, DISC, Big Five) are used by an estimated 1.5–2 million people a year and by the large majority of big employers. That popularity reflects real, documented value — but the evidence is specific about which value:

A. The proven benefit is awareness & communication — not selection.
The consistent finding is that knowing your own and the other person's type improves communication, reduces conflict, and builds empathy — because it gives people a shared language and reframes "style clashes" as differences rather than faults. Expert guidance is equally consistent that these tools should not be used to select or assign people: their predictive validity for that is weak, and type isn't stable enough to bank on. (Myers-Briggs Co.; SHRM-approved practitioner guidance 2026)
B. Personality similarity does help relationships and groups form and bond.
In a year-long study of real friendship groups, similarity in personality predicted both group formation and group success, even after controlling for individuals' own traits. So homophily is a real, positive force in the kind of voluntary, peer-like bond we're building — which supports personality mattering in matching, modestly. (Homophily in Personality Enhances Group Success, 2020)
C. Similarity vs. complementarity is trait-dependent — not one rule.
Co-worker-preference research shows people want similarity on some traits and complementarity on others: we gravitate to those similar in affiliation/warmth, but often pair well with a complement on dominance (a less-dominant person prefers a more-directive counterpart). This is the sophisticated answer to Cynthia's "closest vs. compatible" question — it depends which letter. (Personality & Situations in Co-worker Preference, J. Business & Psychology)

What the youth-mentoring research says

This is our closest domain (Rhodes lab / BBBS datasets / MENTOR's Elements of Effective Practice). Three points frame where personality fits among the other signals:

  • Relationship quality & the early "click" drive outcomes more than any matched trait; a weak early bond predicts early — and potentially harmful — closure. This is the strongest argument for weighting what protects the first weeks: responsiveness and consistency. (Grossman & Rhodes 2002; Rhodes et al. 2017, N=5,222)
  • Shared interests give a modest, real boost to early rapport — helpful but not load-bearing. (Raposa et al. 2018)
  • Personality-type matching specifically has little direct evidence in youth mentoring either way — the field studies demographics, interests, goals, and relationship quality, not type compatibility.

Read together, the two literatures agree: personality is a genuine relationship enhancer with modest matching value — strongest as a tool the pair uses, not as a heavy selection weight.

What this means for Vescent — two uses, not one

Use 1 · Matching weight
Moderate, and testable
Evidence supports personality similarity helping bonds form (finding B) but cautions against heavy selection weight (A). A moderate weight — bumped from 10% but not dominant — is the defensible read. The closest-vs-compatible rule should be set per letter (C), and A/B tested.
Use 2 · Relationship tool
The bigger, cheaper win
Show the mentor her mentee's type (and vice versa) with a few "how to connect with this person" notes. This is exactly where the corporate evidence is strongest, needs no scoring logic, and directly serves our #1 driver — the early connection. Fits Olivia's admin view and the mentor's onboarding.

Recommended starting point

Proposed v1 starting weights (for debate, not decree):
Responsiveness — gate + 25% 4H Journey — 25% Experiences — 20% Personality — 15% Interests — 15%
Personality rises from 10 → 15% (honoring the intuition, staying within the evidence), plus it powers a mentor-facing "how to connect" card that isn't a weight at all. Experiences rises toward the belonging evidence; Interests trims to its modest real effect; Responsiveness is gated. Every number stays tunable in Olivia's tool.

Two prerequisites make any weight set defensible rather than a guess:

  1. Name the outcome first. Each weight is a hypothesis about predicting something — pick it (match still active at 8/12 weeks, session attendance, or Sharpen well-being change). Nothing is testable until this is set.
  2. Version every weight set + set the personality rule per letter. Since matching lives in Olivia's tool, it should log the weight-version and the closest-vs-compatible rule for each letter, so we can back-test and A/B without re-onboarding anyone.
